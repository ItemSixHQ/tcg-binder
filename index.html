New to the Pokémon TCG? Start Here.

I get asked “how do I even start playing” more than anything else — way more than card values, honestly. So here it is: everything you need to sit down with a friend and play a full game, assuming you know nothing. This is the paper game, though Pokémon TCG Live works the same way. Once it clicks, the Advanced Guide takes it further.

The goal

You win one of three ways:

Take all your Prize cards. You set aside 6 face-down at the start. Knock out one of your opponent’s Pokémon and you take Prizes for it:

  • A regular Pokémon — 1 Prize
  • An ex Pokémon — 2 Prizes
  • A Mega Pokémon — 3 Prizes

Your opponent runs out of Pokémon. No Pokémon in play, none left to play — game over.

Your opponent can’t draw a card. If someone’s deck is empty on their draw step, they lose right there. (If your deck is empty — like my bank account — and you can’t draw a card, you lose. That’s it. That’s the whole rule.) This is also called “decking your opponent out” — it barely ever comes up in a casual game, but it’s a real strategy some decks are built entirely around. If you’re familiar with other TCGs, the term is also called milling.

Most games end this way. The thing to hold onto: the bigger and splashier the Pokémon, the more it hands your opponent when it goes down.

What’s in a deck

60 cards, always. Three types: Pokémon cards — what you actually play and attack with. Trainer cards — Items, Supporters, Stadiums, and Tools, doing everything from drawing extra cards to searching your deck to healing damage. Energy cards — attach these to power up your Pokémon’s attacks.

The playmat: where everything goes

You don’t need a playmat to play. A bare table works fine. But a mat does make it obvious where each pile belongs, which helps a lot when you’re new. This is mine, with the five spots numbered.

  • 1Prize cards. 6 cards, set aside face-down at the start. Each time you knock out an opponent’s Pokémon, you take Prize cards into your hand.
  • 2Active spot. The one Pokémon that’s in the fight right now. It attacks, and it takes the hits.
  • 3Bench. Up to 5 Pokémon waiting their turn. They can’t attack from here, but they’re your backup.
  • 4Deck. Your 60 cards, face-down. You always draw from the top.
  • 5Discard pile (Trash). Used cards and knocked-out Pokémon go here, face-up. Your opponent is allowed to look.

Setting up

TL;DR Shuffle and offer a cut, draw 7 (mulligan if you have no Basic Pokémon), place your Active and Bench face-down, set 6 Prize cards face-down, decide who goes first, fist bump, then flip everything face-up and play.
  1. Shuffle your deck and offer it to your opponent to cut. Do this every time you shuffle. It’s entirely up to them whether they take you up on it.
  2. Draw 7 cards. You need at least one Basic Pokémon somewhere in that hand. If you don’t have one, that’s a mulligan: reveal your hand to your opponent to prove it, shuffle it back into your deck, and draw a fresh 7. Keep going until you draw a Basic. (Some hands really do fight you on this.) Once both hands are set up, your opponent draws one extra card for every mulligan you took, unless they also mulliganed, in which case it cancels out and nobody draws extra.
  3. Place your Active Pokémon and Bench face-down. Pick a Basic Pokémon as your Active Pokémon (the one doing the fighting). You can also put more Basics on your Bench, up to 5. All face-down for now.
  4. Set 6 Prize cards. Take the top 6 cards of your deck and place them face-down in your Prize area. You don’t look at them.
  5. Decide who goes first. A coin flip is the usual way (see Coin flips and dice below). Rock-paper-scissors is fine too. As long as you both agree before turn 1, nobody’s going to argue.
  6. Fist bump, then flip everything face-up. Players usually give each other a fist bump as a small sign of respect. It’s not an official rule and nobody will stop you skipping it, but you’d be the only one who did. Then both players flip their Active Pokémon and Bench face-up, and the game begins.

Coin flips and dice

Coin flips come up all the time: deciding who goes first, and then mid-game whenever a card or a Special Condition says “flip a coin.” Heads or tails matters, so it helps to know which is which.

That’s how I treat my coins. As far as I know there’s no official rule tying heads to one particular side, so if you’re ever unsure, just say which is which before you flip.

No coin handy? Roll a die instead. Even is heads, odd is tails.

How a turn works

Every turn has three parts, always in this order:

TL;DR Draw a card. Do as much (or as little) of your Main Phase stuff as you want, in any order. Attack once. Turn over.

1. Draw. Draw one card. Mandatory, no skipping it.

2. Main phase. As much or as little of this as you like, in any order:

  • Play Basic Pokémon to your Bench (5 max)
  • Evolve a Pokémon
  • Attach one Energy from your hand — one per turn total, not one per Pokémon
  • Use Pokémon Abilities
  • Play any number of Item cards
  • Play one Supporter and one Stadium
  • Retreat your Active Pokémon once, paying its Retreat Cost

3. Attack. Pick an attack your Active Pokémon has enough Energy for, resolve it, and your turn ends. One attack per turn, and once you attack, that’s it.

Turn 1 is different

Read this before your first game.

This is the part that confuses almost every new player, so here it is on its own. (It confused me too, for longer than I’d like to admit.)

TL;DR Going first? No attack, no Supporter — first turn only. Going second? You can attack and play a Supporter right away — though like everyone on turn 1, nobody’s evolving anything yet.
If you go first

Your very first turn only: you draw as normal, but you cannot attack and cannot play a Supporter card. Everything else in the main phase is still fair game — playing Basics, attaching Energy, using Abilities, playing Items or a Stadium, retreating.

In practice: set up your board and pass.

If you go second

None of that applies to you. Despite it also being your first turn, you play a completely normal turn — full main phase, and yes, you can attack.

From turn 2 onward, both players play full, unrestricted turns.

Evolution, explained

Basics evolve into Stage 1s, and some Stage 1s evolve again into Stage 2s — follow the chain printed on the card. Place the new card directly on top of the old one. It keeps everything already attached: Energy, damage, Tools. It’s the same Pokémon, just bigger.

The timing rule: a Pokémon has to have been in play since the start of your turn. Two consequences — you can’t evolve something the turn you play it, and nobody evolves anything on turn 1.

Each Pokémon evolves once per turn (no Basic → Stage 1 → Stage 2 in one go), but you can evolve as many different Pokémon in a turn as you like.

Some cards deliberately break this rule — Rare Candy, and Stadiums like Forest of Vitality. That’s covered in the Advanced Guide.

Special Conditions

Five status effects exist. They only ever apply to the Active Pokémon — the moment a Pokémon goes to the Bench, every Special Condition on it disappears. That’s the single most useful thing to remember here: retreating is a cure.

Three of them are shown by physically turning the card, and because a card can only face one direction at a time, only one of those three can be on a Pokémon at once — a new one replaces the old. The other two use damage-counter-style markers, so they can sit alongside anything.

Asleep
Can’t attack or retreat. At Pokémon Checkup, flip a coin — heads and it wakes up. Card is turned counter-clockwise.
Paralyzed
Can’t attack or retreat. Clears automatically at the start of that player’s next turn, so it only ever costs one turn. Card is turned clockwise.
Confused
Can still retreat, but to attack you flip a coin first — tails and the attack fails, 3 damage counters go on your own Pokémon, and your turn ends. Card is turned upside-down.
Burned
At Checkup, put 2 damage counters on it, then flip a coin — heads removes the Burn, tails and it stays. Uses a marker.
Poisoned
At Checkup, put 1 damage counter on it (some cards make it more). Nothing removes it on its own — you retreat, evolve, or play a card that clears it. Uses a marker.

Pokémon Checkup is the little step between turns where Burn and Poison damage lands and Sleep coins get flipped. New players routinely forget it exists; it’s a genuinely common way to lose a game you should have won.

Two more things worth knowing: evolving a Pokémon clears its Special Conditions too, and Burned and Poisoned can both be on the same Pokémon at the same time as one of the turned-card conditions. So a Pokémon can be Asleep, Burned and Poisoned at once — it just can’t be Asleep and Paralyzed at once.

Evolution: the exceptions

The Beginners Guide gives you the normal rule: a Pokémon has to have been in play since the start of your turn, so you can’t evolve something the turn you played it, and nobody evolves anything on turn 1.

That’s the default. Cards are allowed to override it, and some do:

  • Rare Candy skips the middle step entirely — it takes a Basic straight to a Stage 2. It still can’t be used on a Pokémon you put into play that same turn.
  • Forest of Vitality (Stadium, Mega Evolution 117 — also reprinted in Ascended Heroes and Perfect Order) reads: “Each player’s Grass Pokémon can evolve into Grass Pokémon during the turn they play those Pokémon, except during their first turn.” So a Grass deck running this can play a Basic and evolve it immediately — but not on turn 1, and it doesn’t let Rare Candy break its own rule either. Note it says each player’s: your opponent gets the same benefit while it’s on the field.

The general principle: a specific card beats the general rule. If a card says it can do something the rulebook says you can’t, the card wins. That’s true across the whole game, not just evolution.

Rotation and regulation marks

If you’re buying singles or building a tournament-legal deck, you need to know about rotation. Every card released has a small letter printed on it — the regulation mark — usually near the artist credit or set symbol. Once a year, the oldest regulation marks get rotated out of the Standard format, so the format keeps refreshing instead of accumulating years of cards.

The current rotation took effect this year — regulation mark G and everything older is no longer Standard-legal, and only cards marked H, I, and J are tournament-legal in Standard right now. Practically, that means anything from Twilight Masquerade onward is safe to build around, and anything from the original Scarlet & Violet base set through Paradox Rift just rotated out. New sets keep adding new regulation marks as they release, and each stays legal until it’s old enough to rotate out in turn.

Here’s exactly what that mark looks like on two real cards from my own binder — a small letter in a box, bottom-left, near the illustrator credit:

Regulation mark H — e.g. Dudunsparce (Prismatic Evolutions)

Regulation mark J — e.g. Mega Chandelure ex

Keep this in mind if you’re investing in Standard-legal cards: regulation mark H is the oldest of the three currently legal, and following the usual annual pattern, it’s expected to rotate out around April next year. Once that happens, only I and J (plus whatever’s newest by then) will remain Standard-legal.

One more wrinkle: legality is tracked per printing, not just per card name. A lot of Trainer and Pokémon cards get reprinted — sometimes with the exact same art, sometimes with new art — in later sets, and most of the time that doesn’t rescue your older copy: a rotated-out mark is still rotated out by default. There’s a genuine exception — when a later printing carries the exact same name and the exact same text, the older, rotated-mark printing can be legal too — but that only applies to that specific, checked pairing, not to “a reprint exists somewhere.” So don’t assume from the name alone: check the actual regulation mark on the specific copy you own, and if you’re not sure, run it through the Rotation Checker.

If you’re just playing casually with friends, none of this matters — play with whatever cards you own. It only bites you if you’re building for an actual Standard tournament.

ACE SPEC cards

Some Trainer and Energy cards carry an ACE SPEC banner running diagonally down the side. Whatever it is — Item, Tool, or Energy — you can only run one ACE SPEC card in your entire deck, on top of the normal 4-copy limit. They exist because the effect is usually strong enough that the format wouldn’t be balanced without that cap.

On rotation: ACE SPEC isn’t its own block that rotates all at once — each ACE SPEC card rotates exactly like any other card, based on the regulation mark printed on it. The three below are all marked H, so if H rotates out around April next year as expected (see above), these three specific cards go with it — but newer ACE SPECs printed under I, J, or later marks won’t be affected by that.

Prize mapping

You need six Prizes to win. Different Pokémon are worth different amounts — a regular Pokémon is 1, an ex is 2, a Mega is 3. Prize mapping is just working out, in advance, which knockouts add up to six.

Do the maths before you attack, not after. A few common shapes:

  • 2–2–2 — three knockouts on two-Prize Pokémon and the game’s over. The cleanest map, and the one most decks are built around.
  • 1–1–2–2 — pick off two small Pokémon first, then take two big ones. Slower, but it lets you attack whatever’s actually in front of you.
  • 3–3 — two Mega knockouts. Rare, but it happens.

Why it matters: it changes what you attack. If knocking out the big threat in front of you puts your opponent on exactly six, and you could instead knock out something small and set up a clean two-turn finish, the small one is often the right play. Deciding that mid-attack is how games get lost.

The flip side is the prize trade — what your opponent gets back. Attacking with a one-Prize Pokémon into their two-Prize one is a good trade even if it takes an extra turn, because when they knock you out they only get one back.

Sequencing

Same cards, same turn, different order — different outcome. That’s sequencing, and it’s probably the single biggest gap between a casual player and a good one.

Three rules of thumb cover most of it:

  • Draw before you search. Play your draw Supporter first, then your Ultra Ball. Once you’ve seen the extra cards you know what you actually need — searching first means guessing.
  • Commit last. Attach your Energy at the end of the turn, not the start. Don’t bench a Pokémon until you need it — on the Bench it’s a target, in your hand it’s information your opponent doesn’t have.
  • Disrupt before you reveal. If you’re playing something that forces your opponent to make a choice, play it before you show them your full board, so they have to choose blind.

The habit to build: before each card, ask “does playing this tell me something I don’t know yet, or does it lock me in?” Do the first kind first.

Sequencing is one of the things I’m still actively getting better at. Knowing what your cards do is one thing. Doing everything in the least stupid order is apparently another.

Building a deck

Everything above this point is about playing the hand you’re dealt. This part is about building the deck in the first place — a different skill, and honestly the one that separates good players from great ones.

Start with the question that matters before any other: how does this deck actually win? Not “which cards are strong,” but the actual sequence of things that has to happen for you to take six Prizes before your opponent does. Every card in the deck should be answering to that plan. If a card doesn’t help you execute it, it’s taking up a slot that could.

That game plan is what people mean by a deck’s archetype. It’s not the Pokémon on the box art — it’s the shape of how the deck wins. Four broad shapes cover most of them:

Aggro / Pressure
Hit hard and hit early, and don’t give your opponent room to set up. Wins fast or not at all — if the game goes long, this deck is usually losing.
Control
Slow the game down. Disrupt your opponent’s hand, deny resources, and stall until your own late-game pieces are online. Patient by design.
Combo / Setup
Spend the early turns assembling a specific engine or board state, then win in a burst once it’s in place. Weak before the pieces connect, dangerous after.
Midrange / Tempo
A bit of everything — solid attackers, enough disruption to survive an aggro deck, enough speed to punish a control deck. Wins by not being bad at anything.

Real decks rarely sit cleanly in one box. A lot of good Standard lists are aggro decks with just enough disruption to survive round one, or control decks with an attacker sharp enough to close a game once they’ve stabilised. Think of the four as directions, not categories — useful for describing what a deck is trying to do, not a form you have to fill in.

Once you know the plan, the deck itself breaks down into a few jobs:

  • The main attacker(s). The card, or two, actually doing the damage that wins the game. Everything else in the deck exists to get this card into play, keep it in play, and keep it fed.
  • The engine. The Pokémon and Trainers that don’t attack but make the deck run — draw Supporters, search Items, Pokémon that dig for what you need. A deck with a great attacker and a weak engine tends to lose to a deck with an average attacker and a great one.
  • Energy. Not just “add Energy until it feels like enough.” Energy is a resource you’re spending to power specific attacks on specific turns — how much you run, and which types, depends on what your attackers actually cost and how fast your engine can find it. Too little and you can’t attack on curve; too much and you’re holding dead cards.
  • Mobility, recovery, disruption and tech. Switch effects and Retreat support to get a damaged attacker out of the Active spot; recovery to bring back a knocked-out threat or a used-up resource; disruption to slow your opponent down; and a small number of tech cards aimed at specific problem matchups rather than the format in general.

This is where a common beginner mistake shows up: judging a deck by how strong its individual cards are, instead of how well they work together. A 60-card deck isn’t 60 individually good cards, it’s a system — and a system with one real gap (no draw power, no way to recover from a bad opening hand, no answer to a Stadium) loses to a worse-on-paper deck that doesn’t have that gap. Consistency usually beats power. A deck that reliably does 80% of its plan beats a deck that occasionally does 100% of it and falls apart the rest of the time.

You’ll see suggested ratios thrown around — some specific split of Pokémon, Trainers and Energy that’s supposed to be “correct.” Treat those as a starting point, not a rule. The right split depends on what your specific deck is trying to do — an aggro deck built around one cheap attacker needs far less Energy diversity than a deck running several attacker types, and a combo deck might run more Trainers than either. Build toward your own game plan, then adjust based on what actually goes wrong when you play it — not toward a number someone else decided was standard.

It also helps to think of the deck in two parts: the core and the flex slots. The core is the handful of cards the deck can’t function without — your main attacker line, the specific draw and search that makes the engine work. The flex slots (sometimes called tech slots) are what’s left once the core and Energy are accounted for, and that’s where you get to make choices — a card for a specific bad matchup, an extra copy of a switch effect, a second attacker for range. Most of a decklist is fixed the moment you know the archetype; the flex slots are where the deck becomes yours.

Testing a deck is where all of this gets proven or disproven. Play it, and when it loses, work out specifically what it failed to do — did you not draw an attacker in time, run out of Energy, have no way to deal with a Stadium, brick on your opening hand more than once in a session? Then change a card to answer that specific failure. Swapping cards because something “felt off,” without being able to name what actually went wrong, is how people end up with a worse deck after ten rounds of tweaking than they started with.

Your first tournament

If you’re heading to a league night or a local event, a few things catch people out the first time:

  • Deck list. You write down all 60 cards and hand it in before you play. It stops anyone swapping decks mid-event, and once it’s submitted, that’s your deck for the day.
  • Deck check. Judges can pull your deck and count it against your list. Your list has to match what’s in your hands — a mismatch is where penalties come from, and they range from a warning up to a game loss depending on how serious it is.
  • Sleeves. Every card in the deck needs identical sleeves, in good condition. Scuffed, bent or otherwise distinguishable sleeves can be treated as marked cards, which is taken seriously because it looks like cheating whether or not you meant it. Bring a spare pack.
  • Shuffling and cutting. Shuffle properly, then offer your deck to your opponent to cut. Do it every time. It costs you nothing and it’s the norm.
  • Check your own legality. Nobody else is responsible for your deck being format-legal. Check the regulation marks yourself before you go — see the rotation section above.

Beyond that, ask. Judges would far rather answer a question than issue a penalty, and every player at a local event was new once.

    Rotation Checker

    Paste a decklist. I’ll tell you what’s Standard-legal today, what rotates next, and what’s already out.

    Every card printed since 2023 has a small letter in a black box near the bottom edge — the regulation mark. That’s where Standard legality starts — if the mark on your printing isn’t on the current list, it’s not legal, with one carefully-checked exception.

    The exception: when the exact same card — same name, same wording, not just the same art — has also been printed under a mark that’s still legal, your older printing counts as legal too. I only mark it that way once I’ve verified the newer printing’s text matches; I don’t guess from the card’s name.

    How to read the result

    • Legal — fine to play in Standard today, and not first in line to rotate.
    • Rotates next — legal right now, but it’s the oldest mark still standing. When the next rotation happens, this is what goes. Don’t build a new deck around these if you’re planning to play competitively for the next year.
    • Not legal — already rotated out of Standard. Still perfectly good for Expanded, for casual games, and for your binder.
    • Reprinted under a current mark — your printing carries an old, rotated mark, but the exact same card (same name, same text) has been reprinted under a mark that’s legal today, so it’s legal too. This only applies to specific verified pairs, not every card that shares a name with something newer.
    • Check the card — promos and a few odd printings don’t share one mark across the whole set. Look at the actual card in your hand.

    The bit I have to say out loud

    This is a tool I maintain by hand, on a website I run at night. It is not official, it is not affiliated with Pokémon, and it can be out of date — particularly in the few weeks after a new set lands.

    If you are travelling to a tournament, check the official Play! Pokémon rules and formats page before you go. Use this to catch problems early, not to settle an argument at a judge’s table.

    One more thing it can’t do: it reads set codes, not individual cards. If a specific card has been banned on its own, or a printing carries a different mark to the rest of its set, this won’t know. It also doesn’t check the one-ACE-SPEC-per-deck rule.

    Cards you already own

    I found my old cards from the 90s. Am I sitting on money?

    Almost certainly not, and I’d rather tell you that now than let you spend a weekend hoping.

    The vast majority of cards from any era are worth well under a dollar. What carries value is a short list: first edition and shadowless Base Set, a handful of specific holos in genuinely clean condition, sealed product, and a few promos. Condition matters more than age — a played Base Set Charizard with white edges and a soft crease is a different object entirely from a clean one.

    Check a few against recent sold listings, not asking prices. Asking prices are fiction. And if the whole binder comes to twenty dollars, that’s a normal, common outcome, not a sign you did something wrong.

    Should I get my card graded?

    Usually no. Grading costs money per card plus shipping and insurance, and it takes months. For that to make sense, the graded card has to be worth meaningfully more than the raw one plus the fee — and for most cards it simply isn’t.

    It’s worth considering when the card is already worth a few hundred raw, when it looks close to flawless under a light, or when it’s something you want to keep forever and the slab is really about protection and provenance.

    I won’t predict what grade something will get. I’m not a grader, and people who guess grades for other people are usually about to sell them something.

    How do I know if a card is fake?

    Feel it first. Real cards have a specific stiffness and a faint texture; most counterfeits are either too floppy or too glossy. Then look at the back — the blue is a very particular shade and fakes are usually too dark, too purple, or too washed out.

    After that: the text should be crisp under a phone camera zoom, not fuzzy at the edges. The energy symbols should be sharp. Check the set code and card number against a database and make sure that card actually exists at that number in that set.

    If you have a real card of the same era, compare them side by side. That single trick catches more fakes than any checklist.

    What’s a “swirl” and does it make my card worth more?

    A swirl is a pattern in the holo foil, caused during manufacturing, that looks like a little galaxy or fingerprint in the shine. Collectors like them because every one is unique.

    Does it add value? Sometimes, on the right card, to the right buyer. On most cards it adds nothing you could reliably sell for. I’ve looked at comparable sales for swirled cards in my own binder and priced several of them at exactly the same number as an unswirled copy, because that’s what the actual sold listings said.

    Buying and collecting

    Are booster boxes a good investment?

    I’m going to be unhelpful here on purpose: I don’t think of cards as an investment, and I’m not going to help anyone treat them as one.

    What I can tell you factually is that sealed product is printed in enormous quantities now, far more than in the 90s, and that most modern sets stay near or below retail for a long time. People who did well out of sealed product mostly did well out of a specific set at a specific moment, and that’s not a strategy, it’s hindsight.

    Buy a box because opening it looks fun. If it goes up, nice. If you need it to go up, buy something else.

    Should I buy Japanese cards or English?

    Japanese cards are generally better made — better centring, more consistent print quality — and often cheaper, because Japanese sets are smaller and the pull rates are kinder. If you’re collecting for the art, Japanese is frequently the better buy.

    If you want to play in an English-language tournament, you need English cards. Mixed-language decks generally aren’t allowed in official play, so buy for the purpose you actually have.

    My kid wants to start. What do I actually buy?

    Two pre-built theme decks, or one Battle Academy style box, and nothing else. Around thirty or forty dollars total. They can play each other immediately, out of the box, without you having to understand deckbuilding.

    Do not start with booster packs. Packs are a lottery, not a way to build something playable, and a child who opens ten packs still can’t play a game with what’s inside.

    Then let them play for a few weeks before spending anything else. Most of the enjoyment is in the playing, and you’ll find out quickly whether it stuck.

    Do I need to sleeve and toploader everything?

    No. Sleeve what you play with, because shuffling is what actually wears a card. Sleeve and protect the small number of cards that are genuinely worth something. Everything else can live happily in a binder.

    The most common mistake I see is spending more on protection than the cards are worth. The second most common is using cheap PVC pages that damage cards over years — if you buy one thing, make it acid-free, PVC-free binder pages.

    Playing

    I want to actually play. Where do I start?

    Read the Beginners Guide on this site, then download Pokémon TCG Live and play the tutorial. It’s free, it enforces the rules for you, and you can lose in private for a week without anyone watching.

    After that, find a local league night. In Singapore there are shops running them regularly — BlackLabel TCG is one I recommend and have no arrangement with. Bring whatever deck you have. Everyone there was new once and most of them will happily help you fix your list.

    Or message me and I’ll play you. I don’t mind losing and I definitely don’t mind explaining.

    Why did my deck stop being legal?

    Rotation. Once a year, the oldest cards drop out of the Standard format, and they drop out by regulation mark — the little letter in a box near the bottom of the card.

    The confusing part is that it’s mostly per printing, not per card name — a reprint in a newer set usually doesn’t rescue your older copy. There’s one exception: if that exact card — same name, same wording — gets reprinted with unchanged text under a mark that’s still legal, your older printing can ride along and stay legal too. That’s rare enough not to assume — check, don’t guess.

    Paste your list into the Rotation Checker — it accounts for the reprint cases I’ve actually verified, and it’ll show you exactly which lines are the problem.

    What’s the cheapest way to build a deck that can actually win?

    Pick one archetype and buy only for it. The expensive mistake is buying singles for three decks at once and finishing none of them.

    Most of a competitive deck’s cost is a handful of cards. The trainers — the search cards, the draw supporters — are usually cheap and get reused across everything you build, so buying those first is never wasted money.

    And check what rotates before you spend. Building a deck around cards with the oldest legal mark is the single most common way new players waste money.

    Part 2 · Visiting Singapore, from a local

    Before you land: the basics

    This part I can help with now, because it doesn’t depend on any announcement. A few of these are things most visitors only find out by getting them wrong.

    How things work

    • August weather. Hot and humid, roughly 26–32°C, with sudden heavy downpours that stop as fast as they start. Bring a light rain layer, not a coat. Everything indoors is aggressively air-conditioned, so a light long-sleeve for inside is genuinely sensible.
    • Your credit card is your train ticket. You can ride our buses and trains by tapping a contactless (wave) credit or debit card, or a phone wallet. Visa, Mastercard and American Express all work, and so do cards from overseas. No transit card to buy, no queue at the ticket machine. Tap at the gantry when you enter and when you leave (on buses, when you board and when you get off), and use the same card or phone both times. Your bank may add a foreign transaction fee, so it’s worth a quick check before you fly.
    • Cards and cash. Cards are accepted nearly everywhere. Local payments run on PayNow, which needs a Singapore bank account, so carry a bit of cash for hawker centres and smaller shops.
    • Yes, most of us speak English. Contrary to popular belief, English is the language we use most in Singapore, and you’ll be fine almost everywhere. The older generation can be hit and miss, though, and so can some staff who are foreigners themselves. If you’re at an old-school hawker stall or a heartland shop, keep in mind that the aunty or uncle might not follow English well. You can usually tell once you start talking: slow down, point at the menu, smile, and it works out.
    • Tap water is drinkable. PUB says it’s safe straight from the tap.

    Rules and etiquette

    • That tissue packet is not lost and found. If you see a packet of tissues, a lanyard, car keys or even a mobile phone sitting on a table at a hawker centre, leave it alone. It’s not lost property, it’s not yours to take, and it’s not your table to sit at. That table has been choped. “Chope” is Singlish for “reserved”: we claim a seat by leaving something on it, then go off to order food. Nobody will touch it, so feel free to chope a table yourself.
    • Vaping is illegal. Please leave yours at home. Vapes are banned in Singapore, and travellers found with one can be fined.
    • Smoking. A lot of places prohibit it even outdoors, so check the signs. And please don’t walk and smoke, everyone hates that.
    • No eating or drinking on public transport. That means the MRT and buses. Exceptions can be made for medical reasons or if you’re really feeling faint.

    Start with a hawker centre

    Eat like a local, if you’re adventurous

    There are plenty of guides out there recommending food, so I’ll just recommend that you eat like a local, if you’re adventurous.

    Go to a coffeeshop. It isn’t what you think. It’s more like a food court, except it’s not air-conditioned.

Go to an Economic Rice stall, basically a store where you can see a plethora of dishes displayed, like curry chicken, sweet and sour pork and stir-fried vegetables, and go crazy. You’ll know a stall serves prata if it has a big iron griddle, and they usually have a bunch of curries in their display cabinet.     Condition guide

    Every card is graded honestly on this scale. Anything Moderately Played or below gets a written note under the listing explaining exactly where and how it’s worn.

    M
    Mint — flawless, pack-fresh.
    NM
    Near Mint — minimal to no wear.
    LP
    Lightly Played — minor edge/corner wear.
    MP
    Moderately Played — visible scratches or whitening.
    HP
    Heavily Played — heavy wear, possible small tears.
    DMG
    Damaged — bends, tears, writing, or similar.

    Card flags explained

    Some listings carry small badges for printing quirks that collectors look for. They’re not damage — they describe how the card came out of the factory.

    Swirls
    A galaxy-like swirl pattern in the holo foil, created during printing. Often sought after.
    Holo Bleed
    Holo foil extending slightly past the artwork window onto the border. A printing quirk some collectors prize.
    Misprint
    A factory error — miscut edges, off-centre printing, or ink irregularities.
